I read The Bannock of Idaho after almost fifty f i years since it was written, and in preparation for this new edition, I well remember the first advice I received about such a project. When, in the fall of 1946, I announced to the chairman of my doctoral committee at Berkeley that I wished to write a history of the Bannock Indians, he very gently and diplomatically advised me that the subject lacked real significance, and my pursuit of such an inconsequential topic would render very difficult his department's efforts to find a teaching job for me at a prestigious university. But, being of independent mind, I was determined to proceed-partly because I wanted to satisfy a curiosity about the Bannock as a result of growing up in Pocatello, Idaho, just a few miles from their Fort Hall Reservation. In addition, I was convinced then, long before the 1960s emphasis on ethnic interests and culture, that the history of Native Americans was a significant part of the development of the history of the United States and had for too long been overlooked and neglected. Today, scholars are exploring every facet of the traditions and lives of Native Americans. So much for historical fashion.

Since graduate school, I have continued to plow new ground and to explore ignored and overlooked subjects of my areas of interest. For about ten years in the late 1970s and early 1980s, I was engaged by the Shoshone-Bannock of Fort Hall to research their history and provide documentary evidence that explained early conditions during the reservation period which later supported claims they were pursuing against the United States government. Their suits were successful in winning judgments and financial compensation.

As a part of my contract with the ShoshoneBannock, we agreed that I would write comprehensive histories of the tribes and try to interest a press in accepting the manuscripts for publication. The process has resulted in the production of the follow- ing books: The Lemhi: Sacajawea sPeople (The Caxton

Printers, Ltd., 1979); The Northern Shoshoni (The Caxton Printers, Ltd., 1980); The Shoshoni Frontier and the Bear River Massacre (University of Utah Press, 1985); and Chief Pocatello: The "White Plume" (University of Utah Press, 1986). These contributions will now be joined by the reissue of The Bannock of Idaho.

Having said this, the history of the Bannock tells a remarkable story of a proud and resolute group of Native Americans who have been too often disregarded as "Snakes" and "Diggers" when their history and culture are as distinguished as that of the betterknown Sioux, Apache, or Nez Perce.

The readers of this new publication will discover the use of terms that were commonplace a half-century ago and even used by the Bannock themselves. Fortunately, we are more sensitive now in recognizing the respect due to all races and cultures in referring to them. In addition, in the more recent books listed above I have been able to correct a few misapprehensions about the Bannock. Further research has established that the correct spelling of their famous leader is Tahgee, from which the word, Targhee, has been assigned to a National Forest and even a ski resort.

The historical evidence also shows that the Bannock were not involved in what is now recognized as the Bear River Massacre of January 29, 1863, in which at least 250 Northwestern Shoshoni men, women, and children were slaughtered by some California Volunteers under the command of Colonel Patrick Edward Connor. My book on that episode, The Shoshoni Frontier and the Bear River Massacre, although attacked by a few offended Mormons and pro-military enthusiasts, received a national award from Westerners International. It was also used by the National Park Service as the basis for a report that won National Landmark status for the massacre site. For too long this disgraceful affair has been called a "battle." I attempted to set the record straight, in agreement with Hubert Howe Bancroft, who a century ago declared "Had the [Indians] committed this deed, it would pass into history as a butchery or a massacre."[^1]

With such few exceptions as noted above, The Bannock of Idaho presents, I believe, an accurate and perhaps even a colorful picture of Bannock life and history before and during the reservation period. The University of Idaho Press is to be commended for republishing the book, first issued by The Caxton Printers, Ltd., in 1958, as a part of the history and development of Idaho and the Pacific Northwest.

Brigham D. Madsen 1996

[^1]: Hubert Howe Bancroft, History of Utah (San Francisco: The History Company, 1890), 631-632.
